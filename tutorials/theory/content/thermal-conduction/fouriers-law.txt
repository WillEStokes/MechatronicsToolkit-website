<p>
The law describing how heat moves through a solid was discovered experimentally by French physicist Joseph Fourier in 1822. Fourier observed that heat always flows from regions of higher temperature toward regions of lower temperature.
</p>

<p>
Fourier's Law is the fundamental law of heat conduction:
</p>

<p>
$$
q = -kA \frac{dT}{dx}
$$
</p>

<ul>
<li>\(q\) - heat transfer rate</li>
<li>\(k\) - thermal conductivity of the material</li>
<li>\(A\) - cross-sectional area through which heat flows</li>
<li>\(\frac{dT}{dx}\) - temperature gradient along the material</li>
</ul>

<p>
A large temperature gradient means temperature is changing rapidly along the material, which drives a larger heat flow. While the negative sign produces a positive heat flow, indicating that heat moves in the positive \(x\) direction in our example. Materials with higher thermal conductivity transfer heat more easily.
</p>

<p>
For our cylindrical rod, heat entering a small segment at position \(x\) can be written as:
</p>

<p>
$$
q_x = -kA \frac{dT}{dx}
$$
</p>

<p>
At the other side of the segment, \(x + dx\), the heat flow will be slightly different because the temperature gradient has changed. The heat leaving the segment can be written as:
</p>

<p>
$$
q_{x+dx} = -kA \frac{dT}{dx}\Big|_{x+dx}
$$
</p>

<p>
The difference between these two heat flows represents the amount of heat that is lost or gained in this segment.
</p>
