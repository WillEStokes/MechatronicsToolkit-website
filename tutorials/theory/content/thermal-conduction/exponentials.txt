<p>
Now we have the governing differential equation for temperature along the rod, but why do  exponential's appear in the solutions?
</p>

<p>
An exponential function \(y = e^{rx}\) has the property that its derivative is proportional to itself:
</p>

<p>
$$
\frac{d}{dx} e^{rx} = r e^{rx}, \quad \frac{d^2}{dx^2} e^{rx} = r^2 e^{rx}
$$
</p>

<p>
If we take the governing differential equation \(\frac{d^2 \theta}{dx^2} - m^2 \theta = 0\) and try a solution of a known form with an unknown parameter \(r\):
</p>

<p>
$$ \theta(x) = e^{rx} $$
</p>

<p>
and substitute it into the differential equation:
</p>

<p>
$$
\frac{d^2 \theta}{dx^2} - m^2 \theta
=
r^2 e^{rx} - m^2 e^{rx}
= 0
$$
</p>

<p>
$$
e^{rx}(r^2 - m^2) = 0
$$
</p>

<p>
Dividing both sides by \(e^{rx}\):
</p>

<p>
$$
r^2 - m^2 = 0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad r = \pm m
$$
</p>

<p>
This confirms the exponential term we tried is a solution, and there are two variants that satisfy the differential equation:
</p>

<p>
$$
e^{mx} \quad \text{and} \quad e^{-mx}
$$
</p>

<p>
Because the differential equation is linear (the unknown function \(\theta\) and its derivatives appear only next to the first power) and homogeneous (the right-hand side is zero), any linear combination of valid solutions is also a solution. Therefore the general solution to the fin temperature equation is:
</p>

<p>
$$
\theta(x) = C_1 e^{m x} + C_2 e^{-m x}
$$
</p>

<ul>
<li>\(C_1\) and \(C_2\) are constants determined by boundary conditions</li>
<li>The term \(e^{-mx}\) decays along the rod; the term \(e^{mx}\) grows, which may or may not be physically relevant depending on boundary conditions</li>
</ul>
