<p>
Another way to model heat transfer along the rod is the finite element method (FEM).  
This approach can simulate the transient thermal behaviour of the rod as it evolves with time.
</p>

<div class="image-centre-shrink">
    <img src="./content/thermal-conduction/finite-element-method.png">
</div>

<p>
Each finite element represents a small rod segment of length \(dx\) with cross-sectional area \(A\).
</p>

<p>
The element volume is:
</p>

<p>
$$
V = A\,dx
$$
</p>

<p>
and the thermal mass of the element is:
</p>

<p>
$$
m = \rho V
$$
</p>

<p>
The temperature of the element changes depending on the net heat flow into the element.  
Applying conservation of energy over a time step \(dt\) gives:
</p>

<p>
$$
m c \frac{dT}{dt} = q_{left} + q_{right} - q_{radial}
$$
</p>

<p>
where
</p>

<ul>
<li>\(c\) - specific heat capacity of the rod material</li>
</ul>


<p>
and the three heat flows correspond to:
</p>

<ul>
<li>Axial conduction from the neighbouring element on the left</li>
<li>Axial conduction from the neighbouring element on the right</li>
<li>Radial heat transfer between the rod and the surroundings</li>
</ul>

<h4>Axial Conduction</h4>

<p>
Heat transfer between neighbouring elements is calculated using Fourier’s law of conduction:
</p>

<p>
$$
q = kA \frac{\Delta T}{dx}
$$
</p>

<p>
This expression describes the heat flowing between adjacent elements due to the temperature difference along the rod.
</p>

<h4>Radial Heat Transfer</h4>

<p>
Heat can also exchange radially with the surroundings.  
This radial conduction through the cylindrical gap can be described using the logarithmic resistance of a cylindrical wall:
</p>

<p>
$$
q_{radial}
=
\frac{2\pi k dx (T - T_\infty)}
{\ln(r_o / r_i)}
$$
</p>

<ul>
<li>\(k\) - thermal conductivity of the surrounding material</li>
<li>\(r_i\) - rod radius</li>
<li>\(r_o\) - outer radius of the surrounding region</li>
</ul>

<p>
This expression represents heat conduction through the cylindrical layer surrounding the rod.
</p>

<h4>Transient Temperature Update</h4>

<p>
The element temperature is updated using the energy balance:
</p>

<p>
$$
\Delta T =
\frac{q_{net}\, dt}{m c}
$$
</p>

<p>
where
</p>

<p>
$$
q_{net} = q_{left} + q_{right} - q_{radial}
$$
</p>

<p>
By repeatedly applying this update over many small time steps, the temperature distribution gradually evolves until it reaches steady state.
</p>

<h4>Boundary Conditions</h4>

<p>
Consider the base of the rod has a fixed temperature:
</p>

<p>
$$
T(0) = T_{base}
$$
</p>

<p>
As the simulation progresses, heat flows from the base along the rod and into the surroundings until a stable distribution is reached.
</p>

<h4>Relationship to the Fin Equation</h4>

<p>
As the element size becomes small and the time step approaches zero, the transient finite element model approaches the same governing equation used in the analytical solution:
</p>

<p>
$$
\frac{d^2\theta}{dx^2} - m^2 \theta = 0
$$
</p>

<p>
The finite element model provides a transient solution of the same physical problem.
</p>
